You wake up. And you see it. A number. Above every head.
Kian. That's your name. Seventeen. The number is your chances. Every mistake, it drops by one. It never goes back up. Not even if you say sorry.
So you test it. You snap at Jay, your best friend. Six. Five. You laugh. Then you forget his birthday. Five. Four.
Science says it takes five good moments to undo one bad one.
You miss his game. Four. Three. Two. One. One chance left. He is almost done with you.
Then you look in a mirror. Your own number. Above your own head. Everyone has one for you too.
Your mom's number? Three. So tonight, you call her first. Because chances do not grow back.
She picks up. You say sorry. Her number does not move. Still three. Sorry is just a word. The number does not listen to words.
So you run a test. A real one. You cut in line at lunch. The boy behind you? Eight. Seven. You do not even know his name. It still counts.
You leave your sister on read. Nine. Eight. You are late to class. Every small thing counts. Every single one.
So you try the other way. You hold a door. You help a stranger with his books. You smile at the lunch lady. You wait. Nothing. The numbers do not move. Not one. Good does not undo bad. Good just waits.
That is the rule. Bad drops it in a second. Good takes forever to matter.
Your sister Mia has nine. Nine chances. You borrow her charger. You snap it in half. Nine. Eight.
You eat her leftovers. The ones with her name on them. Eight. Seven. You promise to pick her up after practice. You forget. You are playing a game. Seven. Six.
She stops asking you for things. She stops telling you things. Six chances, and she already guards them like gold.
You watch her number like a clock. Every mistake ticks it down. You want to tell her. You want to warn her. But what would you say? I can see your patience running out? She would laugh. Then it would drop again.
Science says a new habit takes sixty-six days. Sixty-six days to build one good habit.
One bad moment takes one second. That is the math. One second to break it. Sixty-six days to rebuild it.
You start counting your own days. Day one: you do the dishes. No one notices. Day two: you walk your sister home. Her number stays six. Day three: you text Jay. Just hey. No answer. Day four. Day five. Day six.
You keep showing up. The numbers do not move. But something else does. Your mom smiles when you come home. Your sister saves you a seat. Jay reads your text. He does not reply. But he reads it.
Friday. The big game. The whole school is there. Jay is starting. And you want to look cool. So you tell the team his secret. The one he told you in confidence. The one about his dad leaving. You say it like a joke. The team laughs. Jay hears it. He is standing right behind you.
His number is one. One chance left. You watch it happen. One. Zero. The pill does not turn red. It does not flash. It just disappears. Gone. Like he is gone.
He does not yell. He does not hit you. He just walks away. He does not answer your texts. At school, he looks through you. Like glass. Like you are not there.
Zero does not mean angry. Zero means done. Done is quiet. Done does not slam doors. Done just stops opening them.
Saturday. You are on the bus. An old man gets on. His number is one. A kid bumps into him. Spills his coffee. One. Zero. The pill is gone. The old man does not shout. He just sits down. He looks out the window. He is used to it. That is the worst part. He is used to being done with. You want to say something. You want to help him up. But you are the reason someone else looks like that now. You get off the bus. You walk home. Slow.
On your street, a couple argues on a porch. Her number is four. He says one more thing. Four. Three. She turns away. He stands there, hands in his pockets. Nobody yells. Nobody leaves. But something left anyway. You keep walking. Every street has a number falling somewhere. Every house. Every room. You count them as you pass. Eight. Six. Five. Three. You stop counting. You run the rest of the way home.
You check the mirror. Your number was five. Now it is four. Four chances left with the people who matter. You did not lose Jay's number. You lost a piece of your own.
Then dinner. Your mom asks about your day. You snap at her. You are tired. You are scared. She goes quiet. Her number was three. Now it is two. Two.
You watch it drop and you cannot stop it. You say sorry. Sorry. Sorry. The number does not move. It never moves up.
You go to your room. You stare at the ceiling. You have spent your whole life thinking people would always be there. They will not. Everyone has a number. And every number is falling.
Monday. You go to Jay's house. You knock. Nothing. Tuesday. You knock again. Nothing. Wednesday. Thursday. Friday. You keep going. You bring his homework. You leave it at the door.
Day seven: rain. You stand there anyway. Soaked. Day eight: his little brother opens the door. He just shakes his head. Day nine: you leave a note. Four words. I was wrong. Forgive me later. Day ten: you fix his bike chain. It was rusted. You leave it shining by the gate. Day eleven: nothing. You go anyway. Showing up is not a trick. It is not a key. It is just showing up.
You do not ask for a chance. You do not say sorry. You just show up. Day twelve. The door opens. It is Jay's mom. She looks at you for a long time. Then she says: he is in the backyard.
You walk around the side. Jay is shooting hoops. Alone. He sees you. He does not smile. He does not walk away either. He bounces the ball to you. One bounce. That is all.
No number above his head. No pill. Just a ball. Rolling to your feet. And you understand. Zero was not the end. Zero was the end of counting. What comes after zero is not a number. It is a choice. His choice. Not yours.
You pick up the ball. You shoot. You miss. He laughs. Just once. Short. But it is real. You play for an hour. No one says sorry. No one has to.
That night you call your mom. First. Every night now. You walk your sister home. Every day. You do the dishes. You show up.
Your number never goes back up. It stays four. It will always stay four. But that is not the point. The point is: you cannot earn a chance back. You can only earn the next one.
