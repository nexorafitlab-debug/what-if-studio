Your mom slides the toast across the table. She smiles. Eat up... it's a good day. But above her head, grey words float. "I'M SORRY I MISSED YOUR GAME." You watch them fade. She never says them. She never will. What is she sorry for? She missed your game last Tuesday. She was working late. Six days she has carried it. And now it is gone. Your name is Eli. You are sixteen. And you can see the words people swallow. Every unsaid sentence floats above their head. Grey text. Soft. Like smoke. It hangs for a few seconds. Then it fades. Once a word fades, it is gone forever. You cannot make anyone say them. You can only watch... You can watch a whole life... disappear in grey letters. Once, just once, you saw a word turn gold. A girl told her friend she was sorry. The grey word turned gold and floated up, like light. So what if you could see the words people swallow? At school, Mr. Hale points at the board. His red pen shakes. Above his head: "I'M AFRAID OF LOSING MY JOB." He says: Detention. Cole shoves a kid in the hallway. Everyone laughs. Above his head: "MY DAD HITS ME." Do you tell him? You don't. Nobody laughs at that. June sits two rows ahead. She draws birds in her notebook. Above her head: "I LIKE YOU." Do you tell her you saw it? You don't. She never looks back. Theo, your best friend since you were six, cracks a joke. Everyone laughs. Above his head: "I MISS MY MOM." His mom moved away. He never talks about it. You stop laughing. On the bus, a man sits beside his wife. Above his head: "I DON'T LOVE YOU ANYMORE." It fades... he swallows it. She never knows. You feel sick. At lunch, you count the words. How many today? Forty-three. All gone by dinner. You stop talking to people. Why bother? You already know what they won't say. You sit alone. You carry a notebook. You write down every word you see. The apology your mom never gave. The thank you your neighbour never said. The confession your best friend never made. You tell yourself you are preserving them. You are not. You are just watching them disappear. The notebook grows thick. Your hands shake. One night, you catch yourself in the bathroom mirror. Above YOUR head, grey words float. "I'M SORRY." "I'M SCARED." "I LOVE YOU." Dozens of them. Stacked. Fading. You have been swallowing your own words... for years. And nobody can see them. Except you... in the mirror. The rule was never one-sided. You were just the last to know. Your dad comes home at midnight. Warehouse nights. He opens the fridge. He looks at you. How was school? Fine. Above his head, the same words as always: "I'M PROUD OF YOU, SON." You have seen them a hundred times. He has never said them. Not once. Not ever. Can you make him say it? No. That's the rule. So you swallow yours too. "DAD, I FORGIVE YOU." It floats above your head in the mirror. And fades. Then Grandma Rose goes to the hospital. Eighty-two. The kindest person you know. You visit her. Tubes. Machines. Her eyes are closed. Above her head, grey words float. Slow... fading. "TELL YOUR FATHER I LOVE HIM." What does she want to say? She wants Dad to know... before it is too late. Dad is in the parking lot. He won't come up. He never comes up. You run. Down the stairs. Across the lot. Dad! You grab his arm. Grandma wants to say she loves you. He stares. She can say it herself. No, Dad. She can't. She is trying. And her words are fading. Right now. What if it fades before he gets there? He looks at the hospital. He looks at you. Then he runs. You follow him up. Room 312. Grandma's eyes open. Dad takes her hand. His voice breaks. Mom. I love you. And above her head, the grey words... turn gold. They rise. Like light. Grandma smiles... I know. That night, Dad sits on your bed. He looks at his hands... Eli. I'm proud of you, son. The words he has swallowed for years... finally said. Out loud. Above his head, nothing floats. Because there is nothing left to swallow. You look in the mirror. Above your head: "DAD, I FORGIVE YOU." Would you say it? You do. You turn around... Dad. I forgive you. The words turn gold. They rise. The next day, you tell June. I like you. She blushes. I know. I swallowed it too. You tell Theo. I miss your mom too. He cries... he laughs. You start saying everything. Every word. Before it fades. Is any of this real? The words are fiction. The swallowing is not. Daniel Wegner at Harvard studied thought suppression. He found that trying not to think about something makes it come back stronger. What you swallow does not vanish. It waits. Psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec found that in the long run, people regret the things they did NOT do... far more than the things they did. The words they did not say. The chances they did not take. Nurse Bronnie Ware spent years with dying people. She wrote down their top five regrets. Number one: I wish I'd lived a life true to myself. Number two: I wish I hadn't worked so hard. Number three: I wish I'd had the courage to express my feelings. Number four: I wish I'd stayed in touch with friends. Number five: I wish I'd let myself be happier. The dying do not regret what they said. They regret what they swallowed. The average person has six thousand thoughts a day. How many of them do you say out loud? How many fade... before anyone hears them? Next morning. The bus. A stranger sits across from you. Above her head, one grey word floats. "GOODBYE."
